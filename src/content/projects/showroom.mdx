## The problem

A small dealership photographs its cars where they stand: on gravel, between other cars, in whatever light the day gives. The ads then look uneven next to a franchise dealer's, and a professional photographer for every car is not in the budget.

<BeforeAfter before={p3008} after={s3008} alt="Peugeot 3008" caption="Drag to compare. The left side is the source photograph; the right side is the output of the Showroom engine from that same photograph." />

## The rule: never touch the car

Image generators are good at making a car look better than it is. For someone selling a used car, that is a liability. So Showroom has one rule that everything else serves: **the background is replaced and the car's pixels are left alone.** Paint, wheels, dents and scratches come through exactly as photographed.

The rule is enforced, not hoped for:

- After rendering, the colour of the car is measured against the original with a perceptual colour-difference formula. A drift beyond the threshold fails the image.
- Every output carries metadata that marks it as a composite, in the standard field for that purpose.
- A dealer's approval of a result is tied to the exact recipe and the exact file. Change either and the approval no longer applies.

## The pipeline

  { t: 'Measure', who: 'A check', d: 'The colour of the car must match the original, or the image fails.', gate: true },

**A gateway** takes the request. It is stateless, verifies a signature unique to each installation, enforces a spending cap and keeps a running cost meter.

**Segmentation** runs in Python on a GPU in the EU. Three models each do the one thing they are best at:

- BiRefNet finds the car. It runs on ONNX Runtime with weights pinned by checksum, so the same input gives the same mask.
- SAM 2.1 marks glass from a single click by the dealer, because what shows through a window belongs to the old background.
- A detector finds licence plates.

Requests are signed, sizes are limited and images are held in memory only; nothing is written to disk on the GPU machine.

**Rendering** is deterministic code, not a generative model. It places the car on a studio scene modelled in Blender, adds a contact shadow and a soft ambient shadow, reflects the car in the floor and cleans the colour fringe at the edge of the mask. Golden-image tests hold the result still: if a change to the renderer alters one pixel of a reference output, a test fails.

## Scenes are built, not generated

The studios are 3D scenes I had modelled and rendered with physically based lighting, using freely licensed textures. The engine works out the ground plane under the car and puts the camera in the matching place in the scene, so the room sits correctly around the car in every shot.

## Where it stands

<p class="note">Showroom is in development. The engine, the gateway and the admin module exist and are tested, but it is not yet switched on for any dealership. What is left is running it with a real dealer's daily photographs and fixing what that shows.</p>

The source photographs on this page are freely licensed stock photographs, not a customer's cars. The studio images are unretouched engine output from those photographs.
