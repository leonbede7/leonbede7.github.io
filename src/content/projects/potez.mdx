## Why I made it

I play chess, and I wanted to practise the openings I actually play, from my own files, not a fixed course. "Potez" is Croatian for "a move".

It is the one project here with no AI in the product. I include it because it shows the other half of the job: a finished tool with a clear idea of what it is for.

<Figure src={practice} alt="Potez practice screen with a chess board, a sidebar of training modes and a position from an opening." caption="Practice mode. You recall the moves; the opponent's replies play themselves." />

## How it trains

- **Learn** shows the next move with an arrow and the comments from your file.
- **Practice** asks you to recall the moves.
- **Review** brings back positions that are due, on a simple schedule of lengthening intervals.
- **Explore** lets you browse lines, choose alternatives and keep notes on a position.
- **Challenge** mixes repertoires and ends only when every line has been played cleanly.

The challenge is the part I am fondest of. A complete line played without a mistake or a hint earns a point. Miss a move in that line later and the point is taken away until you play it cleanly again. It ends at 100% of the lines, not after a fixed number of rounds.

<Figure src={challenge} alt="Potez mastery challenge screen showing progress across selected opening lines." caption="The mastery challenge keeps score in a way you can lose." />

## Under the board

There is no backend and no account. Files are parsed in a Web Worker so a large repertoire does not freeze the page; legal moves are replayed with an established chess library; progress is stored in the browser's own database, with export and backup.

The review schedule is deliberately simple, and the project page says plainly that it is not one of the well-known spaced-repetition algorithms. The bundled study lines cite a public opening database and carry real engine analysis for 1,187 final positions.
